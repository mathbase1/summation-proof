Absolute basics: what every symbol means

The letter n tells us where the list stops. The letter k is only a temporary label for the term currently being discussed. You could replace k by i, j or another letter and the sum would not change.

A square
k2 = k × k

For example, 42 means 4 × 4, which is 16.

A cube
k3 = k × k × k

For example, 43 means 4 × 4 × 4, which is 64.

The sigma sign
Σ means “add the listed terms”

It is shorthand for a finite chain of ordinary additions.

Read sigma notation literally
∑k=1nk2=12+22+32+⋯+n2.

The lower instruction “k = 1” says begin with k equal to one. The upper n says stop when k reaches n. The expression k2 tells us to square each chosen value before adding.

The one fact that every finite sum obeys

Suppose Sp(n) means the total of the pth powers from 1 to n. Then:

Sp(n) = Sp(n−1) +np.

Why that rule is true

No formula is being used here. We are only unpacking what the words “running total” mean.

1
Sp(n − 1) = 1p + 2p + ··· + (n − 1)p
Meaning:

This is the total stopping one term before n.

2
Sp(n) = 1p + 2p + ··· + (n − 1)p + np
Meaning:

The total stopping at n contains exactly the same earlier terms, plus one new final term np.

3
Sp(n) = Sp(n − 1) + np
Why allowed:

We replaced the earlier chain of terms by its name, Sp(n − 1). Nothing numerical changed.

The no-gap test

Later, when we claim that a formula F(n) equals one of these sums, we will check two things: F(0) = 0, and F(n) − F(n − 1) equals the exact new term. Those two facts leave no room for the formula to be wrong at any stage. A full proof of that statement appears near the end.

Formula 1

Why 1 + 2 + ··· + n equals n(n + 1) / 2

1+2+⋯+n =n(n+1)2.
First see the idea with an actual number

Take n = 5. Let S be the total.

S = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5

Write the same five numbers in reverse order underneath:

S = 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1

Add down each column:

2S = 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 5 × 6 = 30

There are two copies of S, so one copy is half of 30:

S = 30 ÷ 2 = 15

Now repeat the same argument for a general stopping number n

The pairing proof, one logical move at a time

This proof works whether n is even or odd because we use two entire copies of the sum.

1
S=1+2+3+⋯+n
What this does:

We give the unknown total the short name S so that we do not have to rewrite the whole chain every time.

2
S=n+(n−1)+(n−2)+⋯+1
Why this is still S:

We have not added or removed a number. We have only reversed the order. Finite addition may be reordered because a + b = b + a and brackets may be regrouped.

3
1+2+3+⋯+n n+(n−1)+(n−2)+⋯+1
What happens in every column:

First column: 1 + n = n + 1. Second: 2 + (n − 1) = n + 1. Third: 3 + (n − 2) = n + 1. The amount added to the first entry is exactly removed from the second, so every pair has the same total.

4
2S=(n+1)+⋯+(n+1)⏟n copies
Why the left side is 2S:

We added one copy of S to another copy of S. S + S = 2S.

Why there are n pairs:

The original list 1, 2, …, n contains n positions, so the two-row arrangement has n columns.

5
2S=n(n+1)
Why multiplication appears:

Adding the same amount n + 1 exactly n times is the definition of multiplying n by n + 1.

6
S=n(n+1)2
Why division by two is legal:

The previous line says two identical copies of S total n(n + 1). Dividing both sides of an equation by the same non-zero number preserves equality. Dividing by 2 leaves one copy of S.

Independent algebra check: the formula gains exactly the next integer

Let F(n) = n(n + 1)/2. We check F(0) = 0, then calculate F(n) − F(n − 1):

1
F(n) − F(n − 1) = n(n + 1)/2 − (n − 1)n/2
Substitution:

In F(n − 1), every n in n(n + 1)/2 is replaced by n − 1. The second factor becomes (n − 1) + 1 = n.

2
= [n(n + 1) − (n − 1)n] / 2
Same denominator:

Both fractions have denominator 2, so subtract their numerators and keep the denominator.

3
= n[(n + 1) − (n − 1)] / 2
Factor n:

Both numerator terms contain n. The reverse distributive law says na − nb = n(a − b).

4
= n[(n + 1 − n + 1)] / 2 = n(2)/2 = n
Signs:

Subtracting (n − 1) gives −n + 1. The n and −n cancel, leaving 2.

Conclusion: the formula starts at zero and gains 1, then 2, then 3, and so on. Therefore its values are exactly the required running totals.

Formula 2 — deliberately much slower

Where the sum-of-squares formula comes from

12+22+⋯+n2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)6.
What is not happening

We are not guessing the final expression and then pretending it is obvious. We first find an identity whose left-hand side cancels when many copies are added. That cancellation reduces the unknown square total to quantities already understood.

Why look at cubes when the problem asks about squares?

Consecutive squares
k2 − (k − 1)2 = 2k − 1

The result has only k to the first power. It is useful for sums of integers, but it does not give us a k2 term to isolate.

Consecutive cubes
k3 − (k − 1)3 = 3k2 − 3k + 1

The result contains k2, plus only the simpler terms k and 1. We already know how to add k, and adding 1 repeatedly is easy. That is exactly why cubes are chosen.

First derive the key cube-difference identity from ordinary multiplication

Do not accept the line k3 − (k − 1)3 = 3k2 − 3k + 1 on trust. The next block derives every symbol in it.

Expanding (k − 1)3 without skipping multiplication

Remember: a third power means three identical factors.

1
(k − 1)3 = (k − 1)(k − 1)(k − 1)
Definition of a cube:

x3 means x × x × x. Here x is the whole bracket k − 1.

2
(k − 1)(k − 1) = k·k + k(−1) + (−1)k + (−1)(−1)
Why four products appear:

Each term in the first bracket multiplies each term in the second. The choices are k with k, k with −1, −1 with k, and −1 with −1.

3
= k2 − k − k + 1 = k2 − 2k + 1
Simplification:

k·k = k2. A positive times a negative is negative. A negative times a negative is positive. Finally, −k − k = −2k.

4
(k − 1)3 = (k2 − 2k + 1)(k − 1)
Replacement:

We replace the first two brackets by the equal expression found in the previous line. The third bracket remains.

5
= k2(k − 1) − 2k(k − 1) + 1(k − 1)
Distributive law:

Every term in k2 − 2k + 1 must multiply the entire bracket k − 1.

6
= (k3 − k2) + (−2k2 + 2k) + (k − 1)
Multiplying each bracket:

k2·k = k3; k2·(−1) = −k2. Also −2k·k = −2k2, while −2k·(−1) = +2k.

7
= k3 − k2 − 2k2 + 2k + k − 1
Removing brackets:

The brackets are joined by plus signs, so their terms can be written in one line without changing signs.

8
(k − 1)3 = k3 − 3k2 + 3k − 1
Collect like terms:

−k2 − 2k2 = −3k2, and 2k + k = 3k. Terms with different powers cannot be combined.

Now subtract that cube from k3

1
k3 − (k − 1)3 = k3 − [k3 − 3k2 + 3k − 1]
Substitution:

We replace (k − 1)3 by the expression just proved equal to it.

2
= k3 − k3 + 3k2 − 3k + 1
The crucial minus sign:

A minus outside a bracket reverses every sign inside: −(+k3) = −k3, −(−3k2) = +3k2, −(+3k) = −3k, and −(−1) = +1.

3
k3 − (k − 1)3 = 3k2 − 3k + 1
Cancellation:

k3 − k3 = 0, so those two terms disappear. This produces the identity we need.

The cancellation mechanism

The identity is true for every positive integer k. Therefore it is true at k = 1, true at k = 2, true at k = 3, and so on. We are allowed to add true equalities: if left side 1 equals right side 1, and left side 2 equals right side 2, then the sum of the left sides equals the sum of the right sides.

1 + 1 + ··· + 1 = n

There is one “+1” from each k-line, and there are n lines, corresponding to k = 1, 2, …, n. Adding one n times gives n.

S2(n) = 12 + 22 + ··· + n2

On the right, −3S1(n) + 3S1(n) = 0. Doing the same addition to both sides keeps the two sides equal.

Step 11 — subtract n from both sides

On the right, +n − n = 0. We have now isolated the term containing the unknown square sum.

Multiplying a number by 2/2 does not change it because 2/2 = 1. This creates a common denominator.

2n3 + 3n2 + n = n(2n2 + 3n + 1)

Each term contains at least one factor n: 2n3 = n·2n2, 3n2 = n·3n, and n = n·1.

2n2 + 3n + 1

We split 3n into 2n + n. The first two terms share 2n; the last two share 1. Both groups then contain the same bracket n + 1, which can be factored out.

This expression was obtained by equalities only. No pattern-fitting step is needed for the proof.

Formula 3 — every hidden step exposed

Where the sum-of-cubes formula comes from

13+23+⋯+n3 = n(n+1)22 = n2(n+1)24.
What the final formula is saying

The sum of the cubes equals the square of the ordinary sum:

13 + 23 + ··· + n3 = (1 + 2 + ··· + n)2.

This is not accepted because a few examples happen to work. We will prove that the amount added when the right side moves from n − 1 to n is exactly n3.

Why none of this is merely pattern spotting

The no-gap proof, built from cancellation

A finite number of examples can suggest a formula but cannot prove it for every n. The arguments above prove identities for a general n. The following principle explains, from the ground up, why matching the starting value and every next increase is conclusive.

Claim

Suppose F(0) = 0 and, for every positive integer k, F(k) − F(k − 1) = ak. Then F(n) = a1 + a2 + ··· + an.

Proof of that claim

1
a1 = F(1) − F(0)
Use the given next-step rule:

Set k = 1.

2
a2 = F(2) − F(1),   a3 = F(3) − F(2),   …
Repeat:

Use the same rule for k = 2, then 3, continuing to n.

3
a1 + ··· + an = [F(1) − F(0)] + [F(2) − F(1)] + ··· + [F(n) − F(n − 1)]
Add the equalities:

The sum of equal left sides equals the sum of equal right sides.

Why the divisions by 2, 6 and 4 always give whole numbers

The denominator 2 in n(n + 1)/2

n and n + 1 are consecutive integers. One of any two consecutive integers is even. Therefore their product n(n + 1) contains a factor 2, so division by 2 is exact.

The denominator 6 in n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6

To be divisible by 6, a whole number needs a factor 2 and a factor 3.

Factor 2: as above, either n or n + 1 is even.

Factor 3: every integer n has one of three possible remainders when divided by 3.

Form of nWhich factor is divisible by 3?Reason

n = 3q + 12n + 12(3q + 1) + 1 = 6q + 3 = 3(2q + 1).
n = 3q + 2n + 1n + 1 = 3q + 3 = 3(q + 1).

So the numerator always contains both a factor 2 and a factor 3, hence a factor 6.

The denominator 4 in n²(n + 1)²/4

We already know n(n + 1) is even, so write n(n + 1) = 2m for some whole number m. Squaring gives n²(n + 1)² = (2m)² = 4m². Therefore division by 4 is exact.
