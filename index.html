One formula only · no skipped algebra · the cube choice earned step by step

Where the sum-of-squares formula actually comes from

12 + 22 + 32 + ··· + n2 = n(n + 1)(2n + 1)6

This edition does not ask you to accept “try cubes” as a clever trick. It first shows the same idea working on the easier sum 1 + 2 + ··· + n, then repeats that physical construction one level higher: lengths are produced by the growth of a square, so square areas are produced by the growth of a cube. The cube is then built in three visible, non-overlapping stages before any algebra is used.

Absolute starting point

Before proving anything, we must be completely clear about the sentence we are trying to prove.

Why the counter letter may change

The letter is only a name for a temporary slot. With the same starting and stopping values, Σi2 and Σk2 mean the same list. Later I use x as a neutral placeholder while demonstrating substitution, then use k as the actual counting value. Changing the name does not change the mathematics.

Examples are checks, not a proof

The method before the trick

2. What would a useful derivation have to accomplish?

The key is not to guess the finished formula. The key is to turn a long addition into something whose middle stages disappear.

2A. The square sum grows by one new square at a time

Call the total after the first k square terms have been added Sk:

Sk = 12 + 22 + ··· + (k − 1)2 + k2.

One stage earlier, the final term is missing:

Sk−1 = 12 + 22 + ··· + (k − 1)2.

Subtracting the earlier total from the later total removes every term they share:

Sk − Sk−1
↓ write out both totals
[12 + ··· + (k − 1)2 + k2] − [12 + ··· + (k − 1)2]
↓ all earlier squares cancel
Sk − Sk−1 = k2.

The exact target

At stage k, the amount newly added is k2. We therefore want a simple, familiar quantity whose change from stage k − 1 to stage k contains a k2 term.

It does not have to equal k2 perfectly. Extra terms are acceptable provided they are simpler totals that we can calculate separately.

2B. Why neighbouring changes are attractive

Suppose a quantity has successive stage values Q0, Q1, Q2, …, Qn. The amount added at each stage is “new value minus old value”:

(Q1 − Q0) + (Q2 − Q1) + (Q3 − Q2) + ··· + (Qn − Qn−1)
= −Q0 + (Q1 − Q1) + (Q2 − Q2) + ··· + (Qn−1 − Qn−1) + Qn
= Qn − Q0.
Every middle stage is first added and then subtracted. Only the final stage and the starting stage survive.

2C. See the cancellation with actual numbers

Use the square numbers 0, 1, 4, 9 and 16 as stage values:

(1 − 0) + (4 − 1) + (9 − 4) + (16 − 9).

If we remove the brackets, the middle values visibly cancel:

−0 + (1 − 1) + (4 − 4) + (9 − 9) + 16 = 16.
So the search question is now concrete: can we find a simple growing object whose stage-by-stage additions contain the square areas 12, 22, …, n2? If we can, adding those growth steps will collapse to one final object.
An honest point about discovery: this is not the only possible proof of the sum-of-squares formula. The claim is narrower and more useful: once we decide to exploit cancellation between neighbouring stages, there is a clear, ordered way to search for the simplest object that produces square terms. The next section carries out that search rather than pretending somebody guessed a cube from nowhere.

The missing natural bridge

3. First watch the same idea solve an easier problem

Before using a cube to add square areas, use a square to add ordinary lengths. This smaller example shows exactly why moving to the next shape is sensible.

3A. Grow a square from side k − 1 to side k

The pattern we have actually earned

To add the length-sized terms k, we watched a square grow. A square’s new border is built from lengths, and adding all borders reconstructs one final square.

Why a cube, in one sentence

We use a cube because it is the first simple familiar object whose one-step growth is built from square layers. We are trying to add square areas, so a three-dimensional object made from square layers is the natural next thing to test.

Why not jump to k4? Its neighbouring change introduces k3 terms, which would require a new and harder cube-sum calculation. The cube is the first simple choice that works without introducing a higher unknown sum. It is therefore the economical choice, not a magical one.

What is being claimed—and what is not

We are not claiming that every mathematician must discover this proof in exactly this order, or that cubes are the only possible route. We are proving that, after choosing the sensible cancellation strategy, testing k, k2, k3, … in order makes k3 the first uncomplicated candidate that can expose k2.

4. Grow the cube in three visible stages

Do not begin by staring at a completed shell and trying to divide it mentally. Begin with the smaller cube and enlarge one dimension at a time. Each new piece is then forced, visible and automatically non-overlapping.

At the default value k = 4, we grow a 3 × 3 × 3 cube into a 4 × 4 × 4 cube. The starting cube has 27 unit cubes and the finished cube has 64, so exactly 37 unit cubes must be added. Why this is a rigorous count without a confusing “priority rule”

1
After the first addition, we have one complete rectangular box.

Its dimensions are k × (k − 1) × (k − 1). The first slab is exactly the material needed to change only the first dimension from k − 1 to k.

2
The second slab is added outside that completed box.

It changes the second dimension from k − 1 to k while the first dimension is already k. Because it lies outside the existing box, it cannot overlap anything already counted.

3
The final layer is added above the second completed box.

Before this step the box is k × k × (k − 1). The top layer changes only the final dimension to k, so it cannot overlap the material below.

4
There is no gap.

At the end, all three dimensions are exactly k, so the result is the complete k × k × k cube. We began with the complete smaller cube and added exactly the material needed at each dimension change.

The construction above is already a complete proof. We now audit it by ordinary algebra so the geometric and symbolic routes agree.

4E. Exactly how the next row is produced

This is where substitutions can become confusing, so we separate the current stage from the next stage. The identity is a row-making rule:

For any current value r:   r3 − (r − 1)3 = 3r2 − 3r + 1.
3
The safe substitution rule: when the new value is r + 1, replace every occurrence of r by the bracketed expression (r + 1) first. Simplify only after every replacement has been made.
r3 − (r − 1)3 = 3r2 − 3r + 1
↓ replace every r by (r + 1), without simplifying yet
(r + 1)3 − [(r + 1) − 1]3 = 3(r + 1)2 − 3(r + 1) + 1
↓ only now simplify (r + 1) − 1 = r
(r + 1)3 − r3 = 3(r + 1)2 − 3(r + 1) + 1.

The current row begins with +r3. The next row contains −r3 because the previous stage of r + 1 is r. Those two copies cancel when the rows are added. That is not an extra trick; it is built into the meaning of “current stage minus previous stage”.

4F. Why use the backward difference rather than the forward difference?

Backward form used in the proof

k3 − (k − 1)3

For k = 1 through n, the first previous cube is 03 and the last current cube is n3. The sum collapses to n3 − 03 = n3.

Forward form, also correct

(k + 1)3 − k3

For k = 1 through n, the endpoints are 13 and (n + 1)3, so the sum collapses to (n + 1)3 − 1.

We choose the backward form only because the original sum begins at k = 1 and the clean starting cube is 03. Both versions contain the same mathematics.

4G. Why the identity is useful rather than merely true

k3 − (k − 1)3 = 3k2 − 3k + 1
↓ add 3k and subtract 1 on both sides
k3 − (k − 1)3 + 3k − 1 = 3k2
↓ divide by 3
k2 = k3 − (k − 1)3 + 3k − 13.
We have now manufactured each square term from three ingredients:

1. a neighbouring-cube change, which collapses when all stages are added;
2. a term involving k, whose total is Tn;
3. a constant −1, whose total is simply −n.

This is the precise reason the cube identity solves the problem. It links the unknown square sum to one final cube and to simpler sums we can calculate.

The correction term tells us what is needed

5. Derive Tn = 1 + 2 + ··· + n with the full pairing table

Section 3 showed an alternative square-border derivation so that the cube idea arose naturally. We now prove the same Tn formula again by the forward-and-reversed table method, because this version makes every pair and the division by 2 visible. Define

Tn = 1 + 2 + 3 + ··· + n.

The letter T is only a short name for this total.

Write one copy in increasing order.Tn = 1 + 2 + 3 + ··· + (n − 1) + n
This is the definition of Tn.
Write the same numbers in reverse order.Tn = n + (n − 1) + (n − 2) + ··· + 2 + 1
A finite sum is unchanged when its terms are reordered.
Find the entry in the kth position of the reversed row.n − (k − 1) = n − k + 1 = n + 1 − k
Before the kth position there are k − 1 downward steps from n, so subtract k − 1.
Add the kth column.k + (n + 1 − k) = k + n + 1 − k = n + 1
The +k and −k cancel. This proves every column total, not just the first few examples.
Count the columns.2Tn = n copies of (n + 1) = n(n + 1)
The first row has the n entries 1, 2, …, n, so there are exactly n columns.
Divide the two equal copies by 2.Tn = n(n + 1)2
2Tn divided by 2 is one Tn.
What if n is odd?

Nothing fails. When n = 5, the middle position contains 3 in each copy, so the middle column is 3 + 3 = 6 = n + 1. We are combining two full copies of the list, not trying to pair terms inside a single copy.

Optional but revealing: a second systematic way to discover the cubic coefficients

This is not needed for the main proof. It answers the invention question from another angle.

The change table motivates a search among cubic expressions, because the one-step change of a cubic is quadratic. We do not assume the guess is correct; we will choose coefficients so its change is exactly k2, and the later start-and-change check guarantees the result:

P(n) = an3 + bn2 + cn + d.

Because the empty sum is zero, P(0) = d = 0. Now demand that the one-step change equals the next square:

P(k) − P(k − 1) = k2.
P(k) − P(k − 1) = a[k3 − (k − 1)3] + b[k2 − (k − 1)2] + c[k − (k − 1)]
↓ insert the three already-derived neighbouring differences
= a(3k2 − 3k + 1) + b(2k − 1) + c
↓ distribute and collect square, linear and constant parts
= (3a)k2 + (−3a + 2b)k + (a − b + c).

For this to equal exactly 1k2 + 0k + 0 for every k, the matching parts must satisfy:

3a = 1,   −3a + 2b = 0,   a − b + c = 0.
3a = 1 ⟹ a = 1/3
Divide both sides by 3.
−3(1/3) + 2b = 0 ⟹ −1 + 2b = 0 ⟹ b = 1/2
Substitute a, add 1, then divide by 2.
1/3 − 1/2 + c = 0 ⟹ −1/6 + c = 0 ⟹ c = 1/6
1/3 = 2/6 and 1/2 = 3/6, so 1/3 − 1/2 = −1/6.
P(n) = n33 + n22 + n6 = 2n3 + 3n2 + n6.

Factorising that numerator gives the same n(n + 1)(2n + 1)/6. This route confirms that cubic thinking is a systematic consequence of the one-step-change requirement, not a lucky trick.

We used only ordinary multiplication, the separately proved formula for 1 + 2 + ··· + n, the identity for a difference of neighbouring cubes, and legal algebraic operations. The final product appeared only after solving the equation created by those facts.

Demystifying the final shape

8. Trace the exact origin of n, n + 1, 2n + 1 and 6

The final formula can look invented if we only see the last line. Here is the complete genealogy of its parts.

Cube growthproduced the coefficient 3 beside Sn.
Pairing Tnintroduced the denominator 2.
Common factor ncame from every term 2n3, 3n2 and n.
Rectangle factorisationproduced n + 1 and 2n + 1.

The factor n

After combining terms, the numerator was 2n3 + 3n2 + n. Every term contains n, so we took n outside:

2n3 + 3n2 + n = n(2n2 + 3n + 1).

The factors n + 1 and 2n + 1

They came from factorising the remaining quadratic:

2n2 + 3n + 1 = (n + 1)(2n + 1).

Multiplying the right side back out gives 2n2 + n + 2n + 1 = 2n2 + 3n + 1.

The factor 2 in the denominator

It entered through the separately proved ordinary-sum formula Tn = n(n + 1)/2 and through placing all terms over a common denominator.

The factor 3 in the denominator

The cube identity produced 3Sn. After isolating that term, we divided by 3 to obtain one Sn. The denominator therefore became 2 × 3 = 6.

A useful memory story, not a substitute for the proof

The “2” comes from the formula for the ordinary sum. The “3” comes from the coefficient of the square term in the difference of cubes. Together they make the “6”.

Why the answer is always a whole number

The left side is a sum of whole-number squares, so the formula must produce a whole number. The product on top is always divisible by both 2 and 3. Because 2 and 3 have no common factor other than 1, being divisible by both means it is divisible by 2 × 3 = 6.

Divisibility by 2

n and n + 1 are consecutive whole numbers. One of any two consecutive whole numbers is even, so n(n + 1) contains a factor 2.

Divisibility by 3

If n leaves remainder 0 on division by 3, then n is divisible by 3. If n leaves remainder 1, then 2n + 1 leaves remainder 0. If n leaves remainder 2, then n + 1 is divisible by 3. So one of n, n + 1, and 2n + 1 always contains a factor 3.

Common points of confusion

Why does the counter k disappear from the final answer?

k is a temporary counter used inside the addition. After every value k = 1, 2, …, n has been processed, the only free stopping number left is n. This is similar to saying “add every item in this box”: the temporary item label is gone after the total is formed.

Why are there n copies of +1?

We write one identity for each k-value. The allowed values are 1, 2, 3, …, n, which is a list of n values. Each identity contributes one +1, so their total is 1 + 1 + ··· + 1 with n terms, equal to n.

Why do the cubes cancel?

Every intermediate cube m3, where 1 ≤ m ≤ n − 1, appears once positively as the first term of m3 − (m − 1)3, and once negatively as the second term of (m + 1)3 − m3. Equal positive and negative copies sum to zero.

Why was it legitimate to factor 2n² + 3n + 1?

Factorising only rewrites an expression as an equal product. Multiplying (n + 1)(2n + 1) gives 2n2 + n + 2n + 1 = 2n2 + 3n + 1, so the two forms are exactly equal.

Could there be a different formula that also works?

Any expression that has the same value for every positive whole number is merely another algebraically equivalent form of the same function. The recurrence check shows the value is uniquely determined: start at 1 and add 22, then 32, and so on. There is no freedom to obtain a different numerical total at any stage.

Why would anyone think of cubes in the first place?

Start with the easier problem of adding 1 + 2 + ··· + n. A square of side k grows from the previous square by an L-shaped border made from k and k − 1, so square growth naturally produces the length-sized terms we want to add. For the sum of squares, the terms k2 are areas. Repeating the same physical idea, we grow a cube, because a cube grows by adding flat square or rectangular layers. It is the first simple object whose new material contains whole k by k square layers. The choice is therefore a repeat of a method that already worked, not a guess made after seeing the algebra.

When the current value r is replaced by r + 1, what exactly changes?

Every occurrence of the placeholder r changes, and the replacement is bracketed first. Thus r3 becomes (r + 1)3; (r − 1)3 becomes [(r + 1) − 1]3; 3r2 becomes 3(r + 1)2; and −3r becomes −3(r + 1). The constant +1 stays +1. Only after all replacements are visible do we simplify [(r + 1) − 1]3 to r3.

Why do we put brackets around k + 1 or n − 1?

Because the replacement is one complete quantity containing two terms. The exponent or outside coefficient must act on the whole replacement. For example, x3 with x = k + 1 becomes (k + 1)3, not k + 13.

Core conclusion: the cube is not an arbitrary trick. The easier square-border argument shows the pattern first: to add lengths, grow a square. Repeating the same physical idea, to add square areas, grow a cube. Enlarging that cube one dimension at a time produces three forced, non-overlapping slabs and the identity that drives the complete derivation.
