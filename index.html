Rule: sums and powers of integers

Where the first three power-sum formulas actually come from — expanded rigorous edition

This page derives, rather than merely states, the formulas for one plus two plus up to n, the sum of the first n squares, and the sum of the first n cubes. It now gives several independent routes: visual counting proofs, exact algebraic derivations, telescoping proofs, finite-difference discovery, recurrence certificates and formal induction. Every important equality is justified. No calculus is required.

Part 0

The foundations: what every symbol means

Before proving anything, we need to be precise about the objects we are adding. Throughout the page, n is a non-negative whole number. Most examples use n at least one. When n is zero, the sum contains no terms and is defined to be zero.

1. Sigma means repeated addition

∑ k=1 n f(k) = f(1)+ f(2)+ ⋯+ f(n)

The letter k is a counter. It takes the values one, two, three, and so on, until it reaches n. The letter itself is not important: the image uses i; this page often uses k.

2. A power is repeated multiplication

k2=k×k k3=k×k×k

Therefore, the sum of squares is one squared plus two squared plus up to n squared, and the sum of cubes is one cubed plus two cubed plus up to n cubed.

3. A partial sum is the total so far

We give each running total a name:

S1(n) = ∑k=1nk
S2(n) = ∑k=1nk2
S3(n) = ∑k=1nk3

4. The newest term is the difference of two consecutive totals

The total up to n contains everything in the total up to n minus one, plus one new term. For squares, for example:

S2(n) = S2(n−1) +n2

Subtract the earlier total from both sides:

S2(n) − S2(n−1) =n2

This simple “new total minus old total equals the new term” fact is one of the main engines behind the later derivations.

Proof versus pattern

Looking at several numerical examples can suggest a formula, but examples alone cannot prove that the formula works for every n. A proof must explain why the equality is forced for all permitted values of n.

Part 0B

The proof toolkit: the exact rules that make every later step legal

A rigorous derivation is not just a chain of attractive formulas. We must know what the sum means, why we may rearrange it, why cancellation works, and what would be enough to certify a proposed answer. This section establishes those facts before using them.

✓
Standard of proof used on this page

Every formula is derived from finite addition, ordinary multiplication, distributivity, associativity, commutativity and division by a non-zero constant. Visual arguments are translated into exact counting equations, and every candidate formula is checked against the defining recurrence.

1. The sum is defined recursively

For a fixed whole-number power p, define the running total by

Sp(0)=0 Sp(n)=Sp(n−1)+np

The first line says that adding no terms gives zero. The second says that the new total is the old total plus the newly arrived term. Repeatedly replacing the old total gives exactly one to the p plus two to the p plus up to n to the p.

2. The algebra moves we use

  • Associativity: brackets may be regrouped in a finite sum.
  • Commutativity: finitely many addends may be reordered.
  • Distributivity: a common factor may be taken outside a finite sum.
  • Equality rule: doing the same legal operation to both sides preserves equality.
  • Safe division: we divide only by fixed non-zero numbers such as two, three, four or six.

Nothing later depends on approximation. All formulas are exact identities for whole-number n.

Finite-sum linearity proved by writing out the terms

Suppose ak and bk are two finite lists. Then

∑k=1n(ak+bk) =(a1+b1)+(a2+b2)+⋯+(an+bn) =(a1+a2+⋯+an)+(b1+b2+⋯+bn) =∑k=1nak+∑k=1nbk

The middle step uses only regrouping and reordering of finitely many additions. The same expansion proves that a constant may be pulled outside: the sum of c ak is c times the sum of ak.

Why this matters: it justifies splitting a summed identity into separate sums and replacing the sum of one with n.
Reversing or reindexing a finite sum does not change its value

The map k ↦ n + 1 − k sends the list 1, 2, …, n to the same list in reverse: n, n − 1, …, 1. No term is lost and no term is repeated. Therefore

∑k=1nk = ∑k=1n(n+1−k).

This is not a mysterious new rule. It is simply the fact that finite addition is unaffected by the order in which the same addends are listed.

The telescoping lemma proved with every cancellation visible
Telescoping lemma For any finite list A0, A1, …, An, the sum of the consecutive differences Ak − Ak−1 equals An − A0.
∑k=1n(Ak−Ak−1) =(A1−A0)+(A2−A1)+⋯+(An−An−1) =An−A0

A1 appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign, so it contributes zero. The same is true of every internal term A2 through An−1. Only the two boundary terms survive.

The fundamental certificate: how to prove a proposed sum formula is the only possible answer
Finite-sum verification theorem Let F be any expression defined for non-negative integers. If F(0) = 0 and F(n) − F(n−1) = np for every positive integer n, then F(n) equals 1p + 2p + ··· + np for every n.

Proof. Add the claimed increment identity for k = 1, 2, …, n:

∑k=1n[F(k)−F(k−1)]=∑k=1nkp F(n)−F(0)=∑k=1nkp F(n)=∑k=1nkp

The first left side telescopes; the final step uses F(0) = 0. This theorem is especially strong: once the starting value and every increment are correct, there is no room for a different running-total sequence.

Part 1

Deriving the sum of the first n integers

∑k=1nk = n(n+1)2
The key idea

Pair the smallest term with the largest term, the next-smallest with the next-largest, and so on. Every such pair has the same total, n plus one.

Complete pairing derivation

  1. Name the sum

    Let S be the total we want:

    S=1+2+3+⋯+ (n−1)+n

  2. Write the same sum in reverse order

    Changing the order does not change a finite addition total:

    S=n+(n−1)+ (n−2)+⋯+2+1

  3. Add the two displayed copies column by column

    The first column is one plus n. The second is two plus n minus one. Every column gives n plus one:

    1+n=n+1 2+(n−1)=n+1 3+(n−2)=n+1

    There are exactly n columns because the original sum has n terms. Therefore, adding two copies of S produces n copies of n plus one.

    2S=n(n+1)

  4. Divide by two because the left side contains two copies of S

    S= n(n+1)2

    This is the required formula.

Why odd n causes no problem

When n is odd, there is a middle term. For example, with n equal to five, the middle term is three. In the two-copy argument, that middle term appears once in each copy, so its column is three plus three, which equals six, and six is n plus one. Nothing special has to be patched.

Geometric reason: two identical staircases make a rectangle

A triangular staircase containing one dot in its first row, two in its second, and n in its last has S dots. A second copy fills the gaps. Together they form a rectangle with n rows and n plus one columns. The rectangle has n times n plus one dots, so either half has half that many.

Direct even-n and odd-n pairing, with no hidden middle-term issue

When n is even

Write n = 2m. There are m ordinary pairs:

(1 + n), (2 + n − 1), …

Each pair is n + 1, so

S=m(n+1)=n2(n+1).

When n is odd

Write n = 2m + 1. There are m outer pairs and one middle term m + 1:

S=m(2m+2)+(m+1) =(m+1)(2m+1) =n(n+1)2
Why the two-row proof is preferable: it handles even and odd n in one argument. The case split above is included only to remove any doubt about the middle term.
Rigour certificate: the formula has exactly the right starting value and increment

Let

F1(n)=n(n+1)2.

First, F1(0) = 0. Next, for n ≥ 1:

F1(n)−F1(n−1)=n(n+1)−(n−1)n2 =n[(n+1)−(n−1)]2 =n(2)2=n.
By the finite-sum verification theorem, this expression must equal 1 + 2 + ··· + n for every permitted n.
Where the quadratic form comes from: finite differences rather than a lucky guess

The running totals are 0, 1, 3, 6, 10, … . Their first differences are 1, 2, 3, 4, …, and the differences of those differences are constantly 1. A quadratic expression has constant second differences, so a quadratic is the natural smallest-degree candidate.

Write the most general quadratic candidate:

F(n)=an2+bn+c.

Its new-term difference is

F(n)−F(n−1)=a[n2−(n−1)2]+b =2an+(b−a).

We need this to equal n for every n. Therefore 2a = 1 and b − a = 0, giving a = b = 1/2. The empty total F(0) = 0 gives c = 0. Hence

F(n)=n22+n2=n(n+1)2.

Part 2

Deriving the sum of the first n squares

∑k=1nk2 = n(n+1)(2n+1) 6
The key idea

The difference between consecutive cubes contains a square term. When we add many consecutive-cube differences, the internal cubes cancel. This is called telescoping.

Main derivation from the difference of consecutive cubes

  1. Expand the next cube from ordinary multiplication

    We do not assume the binomial theorem. We multiply directly:

    (k+1)3 = (k+1) (k+1) (k+1)
    (k+1) (k+1) = k2+2k+1
    (k2+2k+1) (k+1) =k3+k2 +2k2+2k +k+1 =k3+3k2 +3k+1

  2. Subtract k cubed from both sides

    (k+1)3 −k3 =3k2+3k+1

    Rearrange so the square term is isolated:

    3k2 = (k+1)3 −k3−3k−1

  3. Add that identity for every k from one to n

    3 ∑k=1n k2 = ∑k=1n [ (k+1)3 −k3 ] −3 ∑k=1nk − ∑k=1n1

    The last sum is especially simple: adding one exactly n times gives n, so the sum of one from k equals one to n is n.

  4. Show the telescoping cancellation explicitly

    The consecutive-cube part is

    (2³ − 1³) + (3³ − 2³) + (4³ − 3³) + ··· + ((n + 1)³ − n³)

    Every positive internal cube is cancelled by the matching negative cube in the next bracket. Only the final positive cube and the first negative cube survive:

    ∑k=1n [ (k+1)3 −k3 ] = (n+1)3 −13

  5. Insert the formula already proved for the sum of the first n integers

    3S2(n) = (n+1)3 −1 −3 n(n+1)2 −n

  6. Simplify carefully, one line at a time

    3S2(n) = n3+3n2+3n − 3n2+3n2 −n

    Put every term over the common denominator two:

    3S2(n) = 2n3 +6n2 +6n −3n2 −3n −2n 2

    Collect like terms:

    3S2(n) = 2n3+3n2+n2

    Factor out n, then factor the quadratic:

    3S2(n) = n(2n2+3n+1)2 = n(2n+1)(n+1)2

    Finally divide both sides by three:

    S2(n) = n(n+1)(2n+1)6

How someone can discover the square-sum formula systematically

The telescoping proof above proves the formula. The following coefficient method explains why a cubic-shaped answer is natural and shows how its coefficients can be found rather than guessed.

Why try a cubic?

Consecutive square terms grow like a degree-two expression. Taking a running total raises the degree by one, so we look for a degree-three expression. This is a discovery method. The resulting formula is then independently proved.

Suppose the total has the general cubic form

S2(n) =an3+bn2+cn+d

The empty sum at n equals zero is zero, so d equals zero. Also, the newest-term rule says

S2(n)− S2(n−1) =n2

Use these elementary differences:

n3−(n−1)3=3n2−3n+1 n2−(n−1)2=2n−1 n−(n−1)=1

Therefore the difference of the proposed cubic is

3an2 +(−3a+2b)n +(a−b+c) =n2

Two polynomials are equal for every n only when the coefficients of matching powers agree. Here is a direct proof for the exact quadratic comparison being used. Move the right side to the left and call the three resulting coefficients A, B and C. Then A n² + B n + C = 0 for every positive integer n. Substituting n = 1, 2 and 3 gives

A+B+C=0 4A+2B+C=0 9A+3B+C=0

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives 3A + B = 0. Subtracting the second from the third gives 5A + B = 0. Subtract those two new equations: 2A = 0, so A = 0. Then B = 0 and finally C = 0. Thus every matching coefficient really must agree. Therefore:

3a=1 −3a+2b=0 a−b+c=0

Solving gives

a=13, b=12, c=16
S2(n) =n33 +n22 +n6 = n(n+1)(2n+1)6

A second telescoping derivation with cleaner end points: use k³ − (k − 1)³

The earlier derivation used (k + 1)³ − k³. The following equivalent version starts at 1³ − 0³ and ends at n³ − (n − 1)³, so the telescoping boundary becomes exactly n³.

First expand (k − 1)³ by direct multiplication:

(k−1)3=k3−3k2+3k−1.

Subtract this from k³:

k3−(k−1)3=3k2−3k+1.

Now sum this identity from k = 1 to n:

∑k=1n[k3−(k−1)3]=3S2(n)−3S1(n)+n n3−03=3S2(n)−3S1(n)+n.

Insert S1(n) = n(n + 1)/2 and solve:

3S2(n)=n3+3n(n+1)2−n =2n3+3n2+n2 =n(2n2+3n+1)2 =n(n+1)(2n+1)2.

Divide by 3:

S2(n)=n(n+1)(2n+1)6.
Factor check: (n + 1)(2n + 1) expands to 2n² + 3n + 1, so no factorisation has been assumed without verification.
Visual proof with odd-number gnomons: every square is built from 1, 3, 5, … cells

Growing a (j − 1)-by-(j − 1) square into a j-by-j square adds one new bottom row of j cells and one new right column of j cells, but the bottom-right corner belongs to both, so count it once. The number added is j + j − 1 = 2j − 1.

j2−(j−1)2=2j−1.

In the collection 1² + 2² + ··· + n², the jth odd layer appears in every square whose side is at least j: the j-by-j square, the (j + 1)-by-(j + 1) square, …, the n-by-n square. That is exactly n − j + 1 appearances.

Therefore, counting all cells layer by layer gives

S2(n)=∑j=1n(n−j+1)(2j−1).

Now separate the known pieces, carefully:

S2=∑j=1n[(n+1)(2j−1)−j(2j−1)] =(n+1)∑j=1n(2j−1)−2S2+S1 =(n+1)n2−2S2+S1.

The identity 1 + 3 + ··· + (2n − 1) = n² follows from the same square layers. Algebraically, each odd number is a consecutive-square difference:

∑j=1n(2j−1)=∑j=1n[j2−(j−1)2] =n2−02=n2.

Use that result and move the two copies of S2 to the left:

3S2=(n+1)n2+n(n+1)2 =n(n+1)(2n+1)2.

Dividing by 3 gives the square-sum formula.

Why this is not circular: S2 appears on both sides because we counted the same unknown collection in a different order. The resulting linear equation 3S2 = known quantity is then solved normally.
Rigour certificate: the proposed square formula gains exactly n²

Define

F2(n)=n(n+1)(2n+1)6.

Clearly F2(0) = 0. Its increment is

F2(n)−F2(n−1)=n[(n+1)(2n+1)−(n−1)(2n−1)]6 =n[(2n2+3n+1)−(2n2−3n+1)]6 =n(6n)6=n2.
The formula begins at zero and adds precisely the next square. The verification theorem therefore certifies it for every n.
Why a cubic answer is expected: the finite-difference evidence and coefficient derivation

The totals 0, 1, 5, 14, 30, … have first differences 1, 4, 9, 16, …; second differences 3, 5, 7, …; and third differences constantly 2. Constant third differences are the signature of a cubic polynomial.

The earlier coefficient section derives the cubic exactly. The table here explains why degree three was not an arbitrary guess: each finite difference lowers polynomial degree by one, and three differences turn the total into a constant.

Part 3

Deriving the sum of the first n cubes

∑k=1nk3 = [n(n+1)2] 2 = n2(n+1)24
The key idea

The nth triangular number is the sum one plus two plus up to n. The difference between the squares of two consecutive triangular numbers is exactly n cubed. Adding those differences makes all the middle triangular-number squares cancel.

This table suggests that the sum of cubes may equal the square of the sum of the integers. It is not yet a proof. The next identity forces the result for every n.

  1. Define the kth triangular number

    Tk =1+2+⋯+k =k(k+1)2

    Also define T zero to be zero, because the sum with no terms is zero.

  2. Find the difference between two consecutive triangular numbers

    T k contains the terms one through k. T k minus one contains only one through k minus one. Therefore:

    Tk − Tk−1 =k

  3. Find the sum of those two consecutive triangular numbers

    Tk+Tk−1 = k(k+1)2+(k−1)k2 = k[(k+1)+(k−1)]2 = k(2k)2 = k2

  4. Use the difference-of-two-squares identity

    For any A and B, multiply the two brackets directly:

    (A−B)(A+B) = A2+AB−AB−B2 = A2−B2

    Reading that equality from right to left gives the difference-of-two-squares identity:

    A2−B2 =(A−B)(A+B)

    Set A equal to T k and B equal to T k minus one:

    Tk2−Tk−12 = (Tk−Tk−1)(Tk+Tk−1) =k×k2 =k3

    So we have proved the crucial identity:

    k3 = Tk2 − Tk−12

    (T₁² − T₀²) + (T₂² − T₁²) + (T₃² − T₂²) + ··· + (Tₙ² − Tₙ₋₁²)

    Every internal triangular square appears once positively and once negatively, so it cancels. The surviving terms are T n squared and minus T zero squared. Since T zero is zero:

    ∑k=1nk3 =Tn2

  6. Substitute the formula for the nth triangular number

    ∑k=1nk3 = [n(n+1)2] 2

    This is the required cube-sum formula.

How someone can discover the cube-sum formula using coefficients

Because the new term is n cubed, a degree-four total is natural. Start with the most general quartic whose value at zero is zero:

S3(n) =an4+bn3+cn2+dn

Require its consecutive difference to equal n cubed:

S3(n)− S3(n−1) =n3

First calculate the required consecutive-power differences:

n4−(n−1)4 =4n3−6n2+4n−1 n3−(n−1)3 =3n2−3n+1 n2−(n−1)2 =2n−1 n−(n−1)=1

Substituting those four differences into the proposed quartic gives

4an3 +(−6a+3b)n2 +(4a−3b+2c)n +(−a+b−c+d) =n3

Matching the coefficient of n cubed, then n squared, then n, then the constant term gives four equations:

4a=1 −6a+3b=0 4a−3b+2c=0 −a+b−c+d=0

Solve them in order:

4a=1⇒a=14 −6(14)+3b=0⇒b=12 4(14)−3(12)+2c=0⇒c=14 −14+12−14+d=0⇒d=0

Visual proof: cube terms are non-overlapping L-shaped layers inside one large square

Let Tk = 1 + 2 + ··· + k. Draw a square whose side is Tk−1, then enlarge it to a square whose side is Tk. The side grows by exactly k because Tk − Tk−1 = k.

The newly added L-shaped region can be split without overlap into a right rectangle of width k and height Tk, plus a bottom rectangle of height k and width Tk−1. Its area is therefore

kTk+kTk−1=k(Tk+Tk−1)=k×k2=k3.
k = 4

The layers for k = 1, 2, …, n do not overlap, and together they exactly fill the final Tn-by-Tn square. Hence the area of the large square is both Tn² and 1³ + 2³ + ··· + n³. This is the geometric content of the telescoping proof.

An independent algebraic derivation from consecutive fourth powers

This route does not use triangular-number squares. It uses the already proved formulas for first powers and squares, showing that the cube formula also emerges from the same telescoping ladder.

Expand (k − 1)⁴:

k4−(k−1)4=4k3−6k2+4k−1.

Sum from k = 1 to n. The left side telescopes to n⁴:

n4=4S3−6S2+4S1−n.

4S3=n4+6S2−4S1+n =n4+n(n+1)(2n+1)−2n(n+1)+n =n4+2n3+n2 =n2(n+1)2.

Why a quartic answer is expected: finite differences

Cube-sum totals have cubes as first differences. Cubes have constant third differences, so the totals have constant fourth differences. A polynomial with constant fourth difference has degree four, matching the expanded formula n²(n + 1)²/4.

The deeper origin

A systematic machine that produces these formulas instead of guessing them

Pairing and geometric insight are elegant, but there is also a repeatable algebraic method. It is the finite-sum version of finding an expression whose consecutive difference is the term we want to add.

Discrete antiderivative principle To sum a sequence ak, look for an expression A(k) such that A(k) − A(k − 1) = ak. Then the sum from k = 1 to n is A(n) − A(0), because all middle values telescope.

Term k

One possible antiderivative is A(k) = k(k + 1)/2.

A(k) − A(k−1) = k

Term k²

One possible antiderivative is A(k) = k(k + 1)(2k + 1)/6.

A(k) − A(k−1) = k²

Term k³

One possible antiderivative is A(k) = [k(k + 1)/2]².

A(k) − A(k−1) = k³
Why finite differences predict the degrees 2, 3 and 4

Define the backward difference of F by ΔF(n) = F(n) − F(n − 1). Direct expansion shows:

Δ(n)=1 Δ(n2)=2n−1 Δ(n3)=3n2−3n+1 Δ(n4)=4n3−6n2+4n−1

Each difference lowers the highest power by one. Therefore, to obtain k, k² and k³ as increments, we naturally search one degree higher: quadratic, cubic and quartic running totals.

A completely systematic derivation using binomial coefficients and Pascal's identity

This method is optional, but it answers the strongest version of “where did the formulas come from?”: it converts powers into building blocks whose sums telescope automatically.

Step 1: define the building blocks

The binomial coefficient “m choose r”, written C(m,r), counts selections of r objects from m objects, where 0 ≤ r ≤ m. There are m(m − 1)···(m − r + 1) ordered ways to choose r distinct objects, but every unordered selection is counted once for each of its r! possible orders. Dividing by r! therefore gives

C(m,r)=m!r!(m−r)!.

Here m! means m(m − 1)···2·1, and 0! is defined as 1. We also set C(m,r) = 0 when r is outside 0 ≤ r ≤ m; that convention lets later sums start at k = 1 without special cases.

Step 2: prove Pascal's identity

To choose r objects from k + 1 objects, single out one special object. A selection either excludes it, giving C(k,r) choices, or includes it, leaving r − 1 choices from the other k objects, giving C(k,r − 1) choices. The two cases are disjoint and exhaustive, so

C(k+1,r)=C(k,r)+C(k,r−1).

Rearrange it into a consecutive-difference identity:

C(k,r−1)=C(k+1,r)−C(k,r).

Summing from k = r − 1 through n makes the right side telescope:

∑k=r−1nC(k,r−1)=∑k=r−1n[C(k+1,r)−C(k,r)] =C(n+1,r)−C(r−1,r) =C(n+1,r),

The final subtracted term is zero because one cannot choose r objects from only r − 1 objects. The particular cases needed below are:

Σk=1n C(k,1) = C(n+1,2) Σk=1n C(k,2) = C(n+1,3) Σk=1n C(k,3) = C(n+1,4)

Step 3: rewrite ordinary powers in these blocks

k=C(k,1) k2=2C(k,2)+C(k,1) k3=6C(k,3)+6C(k,2)+C(k,1)

These are exact. For example, the square identity expands to k(k − 1) + k = k². The cube identity expands to

k(k−1)(k−2)+3k(k−1)+k=k3.

Step 4: sum each building block

S1=C(n+1,2)=n(n+1)2 S2=2C(n+1,3)+C(n+1,2)=n(n+1)(2n+1)6 S3=6C(n+1,4)+6C(n+1,3)+C(n+1,2)=n2(n+1)24

Step 5: show the final simplifications rather than hiding them

For the square sum:

2C(n+1,3)+C(n+1,2)=n(n+1)(n−1)3+n(n+1)2 =n(n+1)[n−13+12] =n(n+1)2n−2+36 =n(n+1)(2n+1)6.

For the cube sum:

6C(n+1,4)+6C(n+1,3)+C(n+1,2)=n(n+1)[(n−1)(n−2)4+(n−1)+12] =n(n+1)(n2−3n+2)+(4n−4)+24 =n(n+1)n2+n4 =n2(n+1)24.
This method is algorithmic: rewrite powers in a basis with known telescoping differences, sum those pieces, and simplify. It explains how the formulas can be generated rather than merely guessed from a table.

Independent verification

Why mathematical induction proves a formula for every n

The derivations above already establish the formulas. Induction gives a second, independent check and is especially useful for proving a formula once a plausible expression has been discovered.

The induction principle

Imagine an infinite row of dominoes. First show the first domino falls. Then show that whenever domino k falls, it forces domino k plus one to fall. Those two facts force every domino to fall. In mathematics:

  1. Prove the statement for n equals one: the base case.
  2. Assume it is true for an arbitrary n equals k: the inductive hypothesis.
  3. Use that assumption to prove it for n equals k plus one: the inductive step.
Why the induction principle is logically valid, not merely a domino picture

Suppose the base case is true and the k-to-k-plus-one implication is true, but imagine that some positive integer still fails. By the well-ordering property of whole numbers, there would be a smallest failing integer m.

The base case is true, so m cannot be 1. Therefore m − 1 is a positive integer smaller than m. Since m was chosen as the smallest failure, the statement must be true at m − 1. The inductive implication then forces it to be true at m, contradicting the choice of m. Hence no failure exists, and the statement is true for every positive integer.

Logical ingredients: a valid base case, a genuinely arbitrary k, and an implication that proves k + 1 from the assumption at k.
Induction check for the first-power formula

Base case, n equals one:

1=1×22=1

Inductive hypothesis: suppose

1+2+⋯+k=k(k+1)2

Add the next term, k plus one:

k(k+1)2+(k+1)=(k+1)(k2+1) =(k+1)(k+2)2

This is exactly the original formula with n replaced by k plus one.

Induction check for the square-sum formula

Base case, n equals one:

12=1×2×36=1

Inductive hypothesis: suppose

∑j=1kj2 =k(k+1)(2k+1)6

Add the next square, (k plus one) squared:

k(k+1)(2k+1)6+(k+1)2 = (k+1)[k(2k+1)+6(k+1)]6 = (k+1)(2k2+7k+6)6 = (k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)6

Since two k plus three equals two times (k plus one) plus one, this is precisely the formula at n equals k plus one.

Base case, n equals one:

13=[1×22]2=1

Inductive hypothesis: suppose

∑j=1kj3 =k2(k+1)24

Add the next cube:

k2(k+1)24+(k+1)3 = (k+1)2[k24+(k+1)] = (k+1)2(k2+4k+4)4 = (k+1)2(k+2)24

This is the claimed formula with n replaced by k plus one.

Putting it together

What is really happening in all three formulas

Accumulation raises degree

Adding a degree-zero sequence, such as ones, produces a degree-one total. Adding degree-one terms produces a degree-two total. Adding squares produces a cubic total, and adding cubes produces a quartic total.

Differences reverse accumulation

If S(n) is a running total, then S(n) minus S(n minus one) recovers the nth term. This is the discrete counterpart of undoing an accumulation.

Telescoping removes the middle

Writing each term as “next object minus current object” causes neighbouring pieces to cancel when summed. Only boundary terms remain.

Why the divisions by 2, 6 and 4 always give whole numbers

The denominator 2 in the first formula

The numbers n and n plus one are consecutive. One of any two consecutive integers is even, so their product is divisible by two. Therefore n times n plus one divided by two is always a whole number.

The denominator 6 in the square formula

Six equals two times three. First, n and n plus one are consecutive, so one of them supplies a factor of two. We also need a factor of three. Consider the three possible remainders when n is divided by three:

Where the factor of three comes from
Remainder of n on division by 3Factor divisible by 3Reason
0nn itself is a multiple of 3.
12n + 12(3q + 1) + 1 = 6q + 3.
2n + 1n + 1 has remainder 0.

Thus n times n plus one times two n plus one always contains both a factor of two and a factor of three, so it is divisible by six.

The denominator 4 in the cube formula

The cube formula is the square of n times n plus one divided by two. Since n times n plus one is even, write it as two times an integer M. Squaring gives four M squared, which is divisible by four:

n(n+1)=2M ⇒ n2(n+1)24 =M2
Common mistake

In general, the square of a sum is not the sum of the squares. For example, (one plus two) squared is nine, while one squared plus two squared is five. The cube formula is a special statement: the sum of the cubes happens to equal the square of the ordinary sum.

Check 1

Why does the pairing proof create n copies of n plus one rather than n plus one copies of n?

The original sum has n terms, so the two aligned rows have n columns. Each column totals n plus one.

Check 2

Why does the sum of one from k equals one to n equal n?

The index k takes exactly n values: one, two, through n. Each contributes one, so there are n ones.

Check 3

What makes a sum telescope?

Neighbouring terms contain equal and opposite pieces. Those internal pieces cancel, leaving only the first and last boundary terms.
